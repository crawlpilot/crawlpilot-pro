# Incandescent light bulb

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[![](http://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b4/Gluehlampe_01_KMJ.png/500px-Gluehlampe_01_KMJ.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Gluehlampe_01_KMJ.png)

*A 230-volt incandescent light bulb with a medium-sized [E27 base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edison_screw "Edison screw"). The coiled filament is visible between the vertical supply wires.*

An **incandescent light bulb**, also known as an **incandescent lamp** or **incandescent light globe**, is an [electric light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_light "Electric light") that produces illumination by [Joule heating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule_heating) a filament until it [glows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incandescence "Incandescence"). The filament is enclosed in a glass bulb that is either [evacuated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum "Vacuum") or filled with [inert gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inert_gas "Inert gas") to protect the filament from [oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation "Oxidation"). Electric current is supplied to the filament by terminals or wires embedded in the glass. A bulb socket provides mechanical support and electrical connections.

Incandescent bulbs are manufactured in a wide range of sizes, light output, and [voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage "Voltage") ratings, from 1.5 volts to about 300 volts. They require no external [regulating equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulator_(automatic_control) "Regulator (automatic control)"), have low [manufacturing costs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing_cost "Manufacturing cost"), and work equally well on either [alternating current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_current "Alternating current") or [direct current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_current "Direct current"). As a result, the incandescent bulb became widely used in household and commercial lighting, for portable lighting such as table lamps, car [headlamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headlamp "Headlamp"), and [flashlights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flashlight "Flashlight"), and for decorative and advertising lighting.

Incandescent bulbs are much less efficient than other types of electric lighting. Less than 5% of the energy they consume is converted into visible light; the rest is released as heat. The [luminous efficacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminous_efficacy "Luminous efficacy") of a typical incandescent bulb is 16 [lumens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumen_(unit) "Lumen (unit)") per watt (lm/W) for a 120V bulb or 13 lm/W for a 230V bulb, compared with 60 lm/W for a [compact fluorescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_fluorescent "Compact fluorescent") bulb or 100 lm/W for typical white [LED lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LED_lamp "LED lamp"). As a result, incandescent bulbs have declined in market share compared to LED bulbs. This has been accelerated by [government bans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incandescent_light_bulb_ban "Incandescent light bulb ban") aimed at reducing energy consumption. These bans have been controversial in some cases.

## History

[![](http://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/31/Edison_Carbon_Bulb.jpg/500px-Edison_Carbon_Bulb.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Edison_Carbon_Bulb.jpg)

*Original carbon-filament bulb from [Thomas Edison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Edison)'s shop in Menlo Park*

Historians Robert Friedel and [Paul Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Israel_(historian) "Paul Israel (historian)") list inventors of incandescent lamps prior to [Joseph Swan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Swan) and [Thomas Edison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Edison) of [General Electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric). They conclude that [Edison's version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edison_light_bulb "Edison light bulb") was the first practical implementation, able to outstrip the others because of a combination of four factors: an effective [incandescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incandescent "Incandescent") material; a [vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum "Vacuum") higher than other implementations; a high [resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistance "Electrical resistance") that made power distribution from a centralized source economically viable; and the development of the associated components required for a large-scale lighting system. However, Joseph Swan's incandescent light bulb pre-dated Edison's and was sufficiently practical that it was actually installed and in daily use in London in 1881.

Historian [Thomas Hughes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_P._Hughes_(historian) "Thomas P. Hughes (historian)") has attributed Edison's business success to his development of an entire, integrated system of electric lighting.

> The lamp was a small component in his system of electric lighting, and no more critical to its effective functioning than the Edison Jumbo [generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_generator "Electrical generator"), the Edison main and feeder, and the parallel-distribution system. Other inventors with generators and incandescent lamps, and with comparable ingenuity and excellence, have long been forgotten because their creators did not preside over their introduction in a system of [lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighting "Lighting").

—Thomas P. Hughes, in *Technology at the Turning Point*, edited by W. B. Pickett

### Early pre-commercial research

In 1761, [Ebenezer Kinnersley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebenezer_Kinnersley) demonstrated heating a wire to [incandescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incandescence "Incandescence").<sup>[7]</sup> However such wires tended to melt or oxidize very rapidly (burn) in the presence of air.<sup>[8]</sup> [Limelight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limelight) became a popular form of [stage lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stage_lighting "Stage lighting") in the early 19th century, by heating a piece of [calcium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_oxide "Calcium oxide") to incandescence with an [oxyhydrogen torch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxyhydrogen_torch "Oxyhydrogen torch").<sup>[9]</sup>

In 1802, [Humphry Davy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humphry_Davy) used what he described as "a [battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_(electricity) "Battery (electricity)") of immense size",<sup>[10]</sup> consisting of 2,000 cells housed in the basement of the [Royal Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution) of Great Britain,<sup>[11]</sup> to create an incandescent light by passing the current through a thin strip of [platinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platinum "Platinum"), chosen because the metal had an extremely high [melting point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melting_point "Melting point"). It was not bright enough nor did it last long enough to be practical, but it was the precedent behind the efforts of scores of experimenters over the next 75 years.<sup>[12]</sup> Davy also demonstrated the [electric arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_arc "Electric arc"), by passing high current between two pieces of charcoal.

For the next 40 years, much research was given to turning the [carbon arc lamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_arc_lamp "Carbon arc lamp") into a practical means of lighting. The carbon arc itself was dim and violet in color, emitting most of its energy in the [ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet "Ultraviolet"), but the positive electrode was heated to just below the melting point of carbon and glowed very brightly with incandescence very close to that of sunlight. Arc lamps burned up their carbon rods very rapidly, expelled dangerous [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide "Carbon monoxide"), and tended to produce outputs in the tens of kilowatts. Therefore, they were only practical for lighting large areas, so researchers continued to search for a way to make lamps suitable for home use.

Over the first three-quarters of the 19th century, many experimenters worked with various combinations of platinum or [iridium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iridium "Iridium") wires, [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon "Carbon") rods, and evacuated or semi-evacuated enclosures. Many of these devices were demonstrated and some were patented.<sup>[14]</sup>

In 1835, [James Bowman Lindsay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Bowman_Lindsay) demonstrated a constant electric light at a public meeting in [Dundee, Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dundee,_Scotland). He stated that he could "read a book at a distance of one and a half feet". However he did not develop the electric light any further.<sup>[15]</sup>

In 1838, Belgian lithographer [Marcellin Jobard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcellin_Jobard) invented an incandescent light bulb with a vacuum atmosphere using a carbon filament.<sup>[16]</sup>

In 1840, British scientist [Warren De la Rue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_De_la_Rue) enclosed a coiled platinum filament in a [vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum "Vacuum") tube and passed an electric current through it.<sup>[17]</sup> The design was based on the concept that the high melting point of platinum would allow it to operate at high temperatures and that the evacuated chamber would contain fewer gas molecules to react with the platinum, improving its longevity. Although a workable design, the cost of the platinum made it impractical for commercial use.

In 1841, Frederick de Moleyns of England was granted the first [patent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent "Patent") for an incandescent lamp, with a design using platinum wires contained within a vacuum bulb. He also used carbon.<sup>[18]</sup><sup>[19]</sup>

In 1845, American [John W. Starr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wellington_Starr "John Wellington Starr") patented an incandescent light bulb using carbon filaments.<sup>[20]</sup><sup>[21]</sup> His invention was never produced commercially.<sup>[22]</sup><sup>[*[bettersourceneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Questionable_sources "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*]</sup>

In 1851, [Jean Eugène Robert-Houdin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Eug%C3%A8ne_Robert-Houdin) publicly demonstrated incandescent light bulbs on his estate in Blois, France. His light bulbs are on display in the museum of the [Château de Blois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C3%A2teau_de_Blois).<sup>[a]</sup>

In 1859, [Moses G. Farmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses_G._Farmer) built an electric incandescent light bulb using a platinum filament.<sup>[23]</sup> Thomas Edison later saw one of these bulbs in a shop in Boston, Massachusetts, and asked Farmer for advice on the electric light business.

[![](http://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/68/Stamp_of_USSR_1634g.jpg/500px-Stamp_of_USSR_1634g.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Stamp_of_USSR_1634g.jpg)

*[Alexander Lodygin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Lodygin) on 1951 Soviet postal stamp*

In 1872, Russian [Alexander Lodygin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Nikolayevich_Lodygin "Alexander Nikolayevich Lodygin") invented an incandescent light bulb and obtained a Russian patent in 1874. He used as a burner two carbon rods of diminished section in a glass receiver, hermetically sealed, and filled with nitrogen, electrically arranged so that the current could be passed to the second carbon when the first had been consumed.<sup>[24]</sup> Later he lived in the US, changed his name to Alexander de Lodyguine and applied for and obtained patents for incandescent lamps having [chromium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromium "Chromium"), [iridium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iridium "Iridium"), [rhodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodium "Rhodium"), [ruthenium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruthenium "Ruthenium"), [osmium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmium "Osmium"), [molybdenum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum "Molybdenum") and [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten "Tungsten") filaments.<sup>[25]</sup>

On 24 July 1874, a Canadian patent was filed by [Henry Woodward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Woodward_(inventor) "Henry Woodward (inventor)") and [Mathew Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathew_Evans) for a lamp consisting of carbon rods mounted in a nitrogen-filled glass cylinder. They were unsuccessful at commercializing their lamp, and sold rights to their patent<sup>[26]</sup> to Thomas Edison in 1879. (Edison needed ownership of the novel claim of lamps connected in a parallel circuit.)<sup>[27]</sup><sup>[28]</sup> The government of Canada maintains that it is Woodward and Evans who invented the lightbulb.<sup>[29]</sup>

### Commercialization

#### Carbon filament and vacuum

*Carbon filament lamps, showing darkening of bulb*

[![](http://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/7c/Joseph_Wilson_Swan.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Joseph_Wilson_Swan.jpg)

*[Sir Joseph Wilson Swan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Joseph_Wilson_Swan)*

[Joseph Swan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Swan) (1828–1914) was a British physicist and chemist. In 1850, he began working with carbonized paper filaments in an evacuated glass bulb. By 1860, he was able to demonstrate a working device but the lack of a good vacuum and an adequate supply of electricity resulted in a short lifetime for the bulb and an inefficient source of light. By the mid-1870s better pumps had become available, and Swan returned to his experiments.<sup>[30]</sup>

[![](http://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/80/Sir_Joseph_Swan_blue_plaque.jpg/960px-Sir_Joseph_Swan_blue_plaque.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sir_Joseph_Swan_blue_plaque.jpg)

*Historical plaque at [Underhill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underhill,_Low_Fell,_Gateshead "Underhill, Low Fell, Gateshead"), the first house to be lit by electric lights*

With the help of [Charles Stearn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Stearn?action=edit&redlink=1 "Charles Stearn (page does not exist)"), an expert on vacuum pumps, in 1878, Swan developed a method of processing that avoided the early bulb blackening. This received a British Patent in 1880. On 18 December 1878, a lamp using a slender carbon rod was demonstrated at a meeting of the [Newcastle Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_Chemical_Society?action=edit&redlink=1 "Newcastle Chemical Society (page does not exist)") though it only worked for a few minutes. Swan repeated the demonstration at another meeting on 17 January 1879 where it worked successfully. It was shown to 700 who attended a meeting of the [Literary and Philosophical Society of Newcastle upon Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_and_Philosophical_Society_of_Newcastle_upon_Tyne) on 3 February 1879. The society's building was the first public building to be lit by electricity. The lamps Swan demonstrated used a carbon rod from an arc lamp rather than a slender filament. Thus they had low resistance and required very large conductors to supply the necessary current, so they were not commercially practical, although they furnished a demonstration of the possibilities of incandescent lighting with relatively high vacuum, a carbon conductor, and platinum lead-in wires. This bulb lasted about 40 hours.

Swan then turned his attention to producing a better carbon filament and the means of attaching its ends. He devised a method of treating cotton to produce 'parchmentised thread' in the early 1880s and obtained British Patent 4933 that same year. From this year he began installing light bulbs in homes and landmarks in England. His house, [Underhill, Low Fell, Gateshead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underhill,_Low_Fell,_Gateshead), was the first in the world to be lit by a lightbulb. In the early 1880s he had started his company. In 1881, the [Savoy Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy_Theatre) in the [City of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Westminster), London was lit by Swan incandescent lightbulbs, which was the first theatre, and the first public building in the world, to be lit entirely by electricity. The first street in the world to be lit by incandescent lamps was Mosley Street, [Newcastle upon Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne), [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom) in 1880.

[![](http://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/93/Thomas_edison_gl%C3%BChbirne_Getty_450854582.jpg/500px-Thomas_edison_gl%C3%BChbirne_Getty_450854582.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Thomas_edison_gl%C3%BChbirne_Getty_450854582.jpg)

*[Thomas Alva Edison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Alva_Edison) with a light bulb from 1883.*

[Thomas Edison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Edison) began serious research into developing a practical incandescent lamp in 1878. Edison filed his first patent application for "Improvement in Electric Lights" on 14 October 1878. After many experiments, first with [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon "Carbon") in the early 1880s and then with [platinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platinum "Platinum") and other metals, in the end Edison returned to a carbon filament. The first successful test was on 22 October 1879, and lasted 13.5 hours. Edison continued to improve this design and by 4 November 1879, filed for a US patent for an electric lamp using "a carbon filament or strip coiled and connected ... to platina contact wires." Although the patent described several ways of creating the carbon filament including using "cotton and linen thread, wood splints, papers coiled in various ways," Edison and his team later discovered that a carbonized [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan "Japan") bamboo filament could last more than 1200 hours. In 1880, the [Oregon Railroad and Navigation Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oregon_Railroad_and_Navigation_Company) steamer, [*Columbia*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Columbia_(1880) "SS Columbia (1880)"), became the first application for Edison's incandescent electric lamps (it was also the first ship to use a [dynamo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamo "Dynamo")).

Albon Man, a New York lawyer, started [Electro-Dynamic Light Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electro-Dynamic_Light_Company) in 1878 to exploit his patents and those of [William Sawyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_E._Sawyer "William E. Sawyer"). Weeks later the [United States Electric Lighting Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Electric_Lighting_Company) was organized. This company made their first commercial installation of incandescent lamps in the fall of 1880, at the Mercantile Safe Deposit Company in New York City, about six months after the Edison incandescent lamps had been installed on the *Columbia*. [Hiram S. Maxim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiram_S._Maxim) was the chief engineer at the United States Electric Lighting Company. After the great success in the United States, the incandescent light bulb patented by Edison also began to gain widespread popularity in [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe); among other places, the first Edison light bulbs in the [Nordic countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_countries) were installed at the weaving hall of the [Finlayson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finlayson_(company) "Finlayson (company)")'s textile factory in [Tampere, Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampere,_Finland) in March 1882.

On 4 March 1880, just five months after Edison's light bulb, [Alessandro Cruto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Cruto) developed a process to create thin carbon filaments by heating thin platinum filaments in the presence of gaseous [ethyl alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethyl_alcohol "Ethyl alcohol") to coat them with pure graphite, and then [sublimating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublimation_(phase_transition) "Sublimation (phase transition)") the platinum at high temperatures. In 1882 at the Munich Electrical Exhibition in Bavaria, Germany Cruto demonstrated bulbs that were more efficient than Edison's and produced a better, whiter light.<sup>[53]</sup>

[Lewis Latimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Latimer), employed at the time by the United States Electric Lighting Company, developed an improved method of heat-treating carbon filaments which reduced breakage and allowed them to be molded into novel shapes, such as the characteristic "M" shape of Maxim filaments. On 17 January 1882, Latimer received a patent for the "Process of Manufacturing Carbons", an improved method for the production of light bulb filaments, which was purchased by the United States Electric Light Company. Latimer patented other improvements such as a better way of attaching filaments to their wire supports.

In Britain, the Edison and Swan companies merged into the [Edison and Swan United Electric Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edison_and_Swan_United_Electric_Company) (later known as Ediswan, and ultimately incorporated into [Thorn Lighting Limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorn_Lighting_Ltd "Thorn Lighting Ltd")). Edison was initially against this combination, but he was eventually forced to cooperate and the merger was made. Eventually, Edison acquired all of Swan's interest in the company. Swan sold his US patent rights to the [Brush Electric Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brush_Electric_Company) in June 1882.

[![](http://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/06/Light_bulb.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Light_bulb.png)

*[U.S. patent 0,223,898](https://patents.google.com/patent/US223898) by [Thomas Edison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Edison) for an improved electric lamp, 27 January 1880*

The [United States Patent Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Patent_and_Trademark_Office "United States Patent and Trademark Office") issued a ruling 8 October 1883, that Edison's patents were based on the prior art of [William Sawyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_E._Sawyer "William E. Sawyer") and were invalid. Litigation continued for a number of years. Eventually on 6 October 1889, a judge ruled that Edison's electric light improvement claim for "a filament of carbon of high resistance" was valid.<sup>[56]</sup>

In 1893, [Heinrich Göbel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_G%C3%B6bel) claimed he had designed the first incandescent light bulb in 1854, with a thin carbonized [bamboo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bamboo "Bamboo") filament of high resistance, platinum lead-in wires in an all-glass envelope, and a high vacuum. Judges of four courts raised doubts about the alleged Göbel [anticipation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticipation_(patent_law) "Anticipation (patent law)"), but there was never a decision in a final hearing due to the expiration of Edison's patent. Research work published in 2007 concluded that the story of the Göbel lamps in the 1850s is fictitious.<sup>[57]</sup>

The main difficulty with evacuating the lamps was moisture inside the bulb, which [split](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_splitting "Water splitting") when the lamp was lit, with resulting oxygen attacking the filament. In the 1880s, [phosphoric anhydride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoric_anhydride "Phosphoric anhydride") was used in combination with expensive [mercury vacuum pumps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_vacuum_pump "Mercury vacuum pump"). However, about 1893, Italian inventor [Arturo Malignani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arturo_Malignani?action=edit&redlink=1 "Arturo Malignani (page does not exist)") (1865–1939), who lacked these pumps, discovered that phosphorus vapours did the job of chemically binding the remaining amounts of water and oxygen. In 1896 he patented a process of introducing [red phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_phosphorus "Red phosphorus") as the so-called [getter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getter "Getter") inside the bulb which allowed the production of economic bulbs lasting 800 hours; his patent was acquired by Edison in 1898.

In 1897, German physicist and chemist [Walther Nernst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walther_Nernst) developed the [Nernst lamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nernst_lamp), a form of incandescent lamp that used a ceramic [globar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globar "Globar") and did not require enclosure in a vacuum or inert gas.<sup>[60]</sup><sup>[61]</sup> Twice as efficient as carbon filament lamps, Nernst lamps were briefly popular until overtaken by lamps using metal filaments.

#### Metal filament, inert gas

[![](http://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ad/Squirrel_Cage_filament_lamp.jpg/500px-Squirrel_Cage_filament_lamp.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Squirrel_Cage_filament_lamp.jpg)

*A tantalum lamp with a 1 meter long filament.*

US575002A patent on 01.Dec.1897 to Alexander Lodyguine (Lodygin, Russia) describes filaments made of rare metals, amongst them was tungsten. Lodygin invented a process where rare metals such as tungsten can be chemically treated and heat-vaporized onto an electrically heated thread-like wire (platinum, carbon, gold) acting as a temporary base or skeletal form. (US patent 575,002). Lodygin later sold the patent rights to General Electric.

In 1902, [Siemens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens) developed a [tantalum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantalum "Tantalum") lamp filament that was more efficient than even graphitized carbon filaments since they could operate at higher temperature. Since tantalum metal has a lower resistivity than carbon, the tantalum lamp filament was quite long and required multiple internal supports. The metal filament gradually shortened in use; the filaments were installed with large slack loops. Lamps used for several hundred hours became quite fragile. Metal filaments had the property of breaking and re-welding, though this would usually decrease resistance and shorten the life of the filament. General Electric bought the rights to use tantalum filaments and produced them in the US until 1913.

From 1898 to around 1905, [osmium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmium "Osmium") was also used as a filament in lamps made by [Carl Auer von Welsbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Auer_von_Welsbach). The metal was so expensive that used lamps could be returned for partial credit.<sup>[64]</sup> It could not be made for 110V or 220V so several lamps were wired in series for use on standard voltage circuits. These were primarily sold in Europe.

#### Tungsten filament

[![](http://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/54/Dr._Just_S%C3%A1ndor_%C3%A9s_Hanaman_Ferenc.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dr._Just_S%C3%A1ndor_%C3%A9s_Hanaman_Ferenc.jpg)

*Hanaman (left) and Just (right), the inventors of the tungsten bulbs*

[![](http://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/7a/Tungsram_advertisement.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tungsram_advertisement.jpg)

*[Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language "Hungarian language") advertising of the [Tungsram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsram) bulb from 1906. The inscription reads: *wire lamp with a drawn wire – indestructible*.*

On 13 December 1904, [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary "Hungary") [Sándor Just](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A1ndor_Just) and [Croatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croats "Croats") [Franjo Hanaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franjo_Hanaman) were granted a Hungarian patent (No. 34541) for a [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten "Tungsten") filament lamp that lasted longer and gave brighter light than the carbon filament. Tungsten filament lamps were first marketed by the [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary "Hungary") company [Tungsram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsram) in 1904. This type is often called Tungsram-bulbs in many European countries. Filling a bulb with an [inert gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inert_gas "Inert gas") such as [argon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon "Argon") or [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen "Nitrogen") slows the evaporation of the tungsten filament compared to operating it in a vacuum. This allows for greater temperatures and therefore greater [efficacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminous_efficacy "Luminous efficacy") with less reduction in filament life.

In 1906, [William D. Coolidge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_D._Coolidge) developed a method of making "ductile tungsten" from [sintered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sintered "Sintered") [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten "Tungsten") which could be made into filaments while working for [General Electric Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric "General Electric").<sup>[67]</sup> By 1911 General Electric had begun selling incandescent light bulbs with ductile tungsten wire.<sup>[68]</sup>

In 1913, [Irving Langmuir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_Langmuir) found that filling a lamp with [inert gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inert_gas "Inert gas") (nitrogen at first, and later argon) instead of a vacuum resulted in twice the luminous efficacy and reduced bulb blackening.<sup>[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]</sup> He patented his device on April 18, 1916.<sup>[69]</sup>

[![](http://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/17/Tungsten_filament.JPG/500px-Tungsten_filament.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tungsten_filament.JPG)

*[Scanning electron microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scanning_electron_microscope) image of a coiled coil [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten "Tungsten") filament*

In 1917, [Burnie Lee Benbow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnie_Lee_Benbow) was granted a patent for the *coiled coil filament*, in which a coiled filament is then itself wrapped into a coil by use of a [mandrel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandrel "Mandrel").<sup>[70]</sup><sup>[71]</sup> In 1921, [Junichi Miura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junichi_Miura?action=edit&redlink=1 "Junichi Miura (page does not exist)") created the first double-coil bulb using a coiled coil tungsten filament while working for [Hakunetsusha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakunetsusha) (a predecessor of [Toshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshiba)). At the time, machinery to mass-produce coiled coil filaments did not exist. Hakunetsusha developed a method to mass-produce coiled coil filaments by 1936.<sup>[72]</sup>

Between 1924 and the outbreak of the Second World War, the [Phoebus cartel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoebus_cartel) attempted to fix prices and sales quotas for bulb manufacturers outside of North America.<sup>[73]</sup>

In 1925, [Marvin Pipkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvin_Pipkin), an American chemist, patented a process for [frosting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frosted_glass "Frosted glass") the inside of lamp bulbs without weakening them.<sup>[74]</sup> In 1947, he patented a process for coating the inside of lamps with [silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silica "Silica").<sup>[75]</sup>

In 1930, [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary "Hungary") [Imre Bródy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imre_Br%C3%B3dy) filled lamps with krypton gas rather than argon, and designed a process to obtain krypton from air. Production of krypton filled lamps based on his invention started at [Ajka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajka), Hungary in 1937, in a factory co-designed by Polányi and Hungarian-born physicist [Egon Orowan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egon_Orowan).<sup>[76]</sup><sup>[*[additional citation(s) needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]</sup>

By 1964, improvements in efficiency and production of incandescent lamps had reduced the cost of providing a given quantity of light by a factor of thirty, compared with the cost at introduction of Edison's lighting system.<sup>[77]</sup>

Consumption of incandescent light bulbs grew rapidly in the US. In 1885, an estimated 300,000 general lighting service lamps were sold, all with carbon filaments. When tungsten filaments were introduced, about 50 million lamp sockets existed in the US. In 1914, 88.5 million lamps were used, (only 15% with carbon filaments), and by 1945, annual sales of lamps were 795 million (more than 5 lamps per person per year).<sup>[78]</sup>

## Efficacy and efficiency

Further information: [Luminous efficacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminous_efficacy)

[![](http://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/47/2200K_Spectrum.jpg/960px-2200K_Spectrum.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:2200K_Spectrum.jpg)

*Spectrum of an incandescent lamp at 2200K, showing most of its emission as invisible [infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared "Infrared") light*

[![](http://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/58/Thermal_image_of_an_incandescent_light.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Thermal_image_of_an_incandescent_light.jpg)

*Thermal image of an incandescent bulb. 22–175°C (72–347°F). Most of the mid and far-infrared is absorbed by the glass, heating it to scorching temperatures. This heats the surrounding air, which rises, helping cool the bulb from the bottom up.*

Less than 5% of the power consumed by a typical tungsten incandescent light bulb is converted into visible light, with most of the rest being emitted as invisible infrared radiation.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[79]</sup> Some applications make use of the emitted infrared radiation, such as [heat lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_lamp "Heat lamp") in [incubators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incubator_(egg) "Incubator (egg)"), [lava lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava_lamp "Lava lamp"), [Edison effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edison_effect) bulbs, and the [Easy-Bake Oven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easy-Bake_Oven) toy. Quartz envelope halogen [infrared heaters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_heater "Infrared heater") are used for industrial processes such as paint curing and space heating.

Light bulbs are rated by their [luminous efficacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminous_efficacy "Luminous efficacy"), which is the ratio of the amount of visible light emitted ([luminous flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminous_flux "Luminous flux")) to the electrical power consumed.<sup>[80]</sup> Luminous efficacy is measured in [lumens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumen_(unit) "Lumen (unit)") per watt (lm/W).

The luminous *efficiency* of a source is defined as the ratio of its luminous efficacy to the maximum possible luminous efficacy, which is 683 lm/W.<sup>[81]</sup><sup>[82]</sup> An ideal white light source could produce about 250 lumens per watt, corresponding to a luminous efficiency of 37%.<sup>[83]</sup>

For a given quantity of light, an incandescent light bulb consumes more power and emits more heat than most other types of electric light. In buildings where [air conditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_conditioning "Air conditioning") is used, incandescent lamps' heat output increases load on the air conditioning system.<sup>[84]</sup> While heat from lights will reduce the need to run a building's heating system, the latter can usually produce the same amount of heat at lower cost than incandescent lights.

| 40W tungsten incandescent (120 V, general service) | 1.9% | 12.6<sup>[1]</sup> |
| 60W tungsten incandescent (120 V, general service) | 2.1% | 14.5<sup>[1]</sup> |
| 100W tungsten incandescent (120 V, general service) | 2.6% | 17.5<sup>[1]</sup> |
| Glass halogen | 2.3% | 16 |
| Quartz halogen | 3.5% | 24 |

### Color rendering

The spectrum of light produced by an incandescent lamp closely approximates that of a [black body radiator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_body_radiator "Black body radiator") at the same temperature. The basis for light sources used as the standard for color perception is a tungsten incandescent lamp operating at a defined temperature.

[![](http://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/be/Spectral_power_distribution_of_a_25_W_incandescent_light_bulb.png/960px-Spectral_power_distribution_of_a_25_W_incandescent_light_bulb.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Spectral_power_distribution_of_a_25_W_incandescent_light_bulb.png)

*Spectral power distribution of a 25W incandescent light bulb.*

Light sources such as fluorescent lamps, [high-intensity discharge lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-intensity_discharge_lamp "High-intensity discharge lamp") and [LED lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LED_lamp "LED lamp") have higher luminous efficiency. These devices produce light by [luminescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminescence "Luminescence"). Their light has bands of characteristic wavelengths, without the "tail" of invisible infrared emissions, instead of the continuous spectrum produced by a thermal source. By careful selection of fluorescent phosphor coatings or filters which modify the spectral distribution, the spectrum emitted can be tuned to mimic the appearance of incandescent sources, or other different [color temperatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_temperature "Color temperature") of white light. When used for tasks sensitive to color, such as motion picture lighting, these sources may require particular techniques to duplicate the appearance of incandescent lighting. [Metamerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamerism_(color) "Metamerism (color)") describes the effect of different light spectrum distributions on the perception of color.

### Cost of lighting

The initial cost of an incandescent bulb is small compared to the cost of the energy it uses over its lifetime. Incandescent bulbs have a shorter life than most other lighting, an important factor if replacement is inconvenient or expensive. Some types of lamp, including incandescent and fluorescent, emit less light as they age; this may be an inconvenience, or may reduce effective lifetime due to lamp replacement before total failure. A comparison of incandescent lamp operating cost with other light sources must include illumination requirements, cost of the lamp and labor cost to replace lamps (taking into account effective lamp lifetime), cost of electricity used, effect of lamp operation on heating and air conditioning systems. When used for lighting in houses and commercial buildings, the energy lost to heat can significantly increase the energy required by a building's [air conditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_conditioning "Air conditioning") system. During the heating season heat produced by the bulbs is not wasted, although in most cases it is more cost effective to obtain heat from the building's heating system. Regardless, over the course of a year a more efficient lighting system than incandescent saves energy in nearly all climates.

### Measures to ban

Main article: [Incandescent light bulb ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incandescent_light_bulb_ban)

Since incandescent light bulbs use more energy than alternatives such as compact fluorescent lamps ([CFLs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_fluorescent_lamp "Compact fluorescent lamp")) and [LED lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LED_lamp "LED lamp"), many governments have introduced measures to ban them by setting minimum efficacy standards higher than can be achieved by incandescent lamps. Measures to ban light bulbs have been implemented in the European Union, the United States, Russia, Brazil, Argentina, Canada and Australia, among others. The European Commission has calculated that the ban contributes €5billion to €10billion to the economy and saves 40TWh of electricity every year, translating in CO<sub>2</sub> emission reductions of 15million tonnes (33billion pounds).

Objections to banning incandescent light bulbs include the higher initial cost of alternatives, lower quality of light of fluorescent lamps, and resistance to government regulation. Some people have concerns about the [health effects of fluorescent lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorescent_lamps_and_health "Fluorescent lamps and health"). A 2017 review in the *World Journal of Biological Psychiatry* reported that blue-rich white LED lighting can suppress melatonin and disrupt sleep and circadian rhythms, with potential implications for mental illness.

### Efforts to improve efficacy

[![](http://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/49/Wolfram-Halogengl%C3%BChlampe.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Wolfram-Halogengl%C3%BChlampe.png)

*Xenon [halogen lamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogen_lamp "Halogen lamp") with an E27 base, which can replace a non-halogen bulb*

Some research has been carried out to improve the efficacy of commercial incandescent lamps. In 2007, [General Electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric) announced a *high efficiency incandescent* (HEI) lamp project, which they claimed would ultimately be as much as four times more efficient than current incandescents, although their initial production goal was to be approximately twice as efficient. The HEI program was terminated in 2008 due to slow progress. US Department of Energy research at [Sandia National Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandia_National_Laboratories) initially indicated the potential for dramatically improved efficiency from a [photonic lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photonic_lattice "Photonic lattice") filament. However, later work indicated that initially promising results were in error.

Prompted by legislation in various countries mandating increased bulb efficiency, *hybrid* incandescent bulbs have been introduced by [Philips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philips). The *Halogena Energy Saver* incandescents can produce about 23 lm/W; about 30 percent more efficient than traditional incandescents, by using a reflective capsule to reflect formerly wasted infrared radiation back to the filament, from which some is re-emitted as visible light. This concept was pioneered by Duro-Test in 1980 with a commercial product that produced 29.8 lm/W. More advanced reflectors based on [interference filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interference_filter "Interference filter") or [photonic crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photonic_crystal "Photonic crystal") can theoretically result in higher efficiency, up to a limit of about 270 lm/W (40% of the maximum efficacy possible). Laboratory proof-of-concept experiments have produced as much as 45 lm/W, approaching the efficacy of compact fluorescent bulbs which is still lower than LED bulbs.

## Construction

Incandescent light bulbs consist of an air-tight glass enclosure (the envelope, or bulb) with a filament of [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten "Tungsten") wire inside the bulb, through which an [electric current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current "Electric current") is passed. Contact wires and a base with two (or more) conductors provide electrical connections to the filament. Incandescent light bulbs usually contain a stem or glass mount anchored to the bulb's base that allows the electrical contacts to run through the envelope without air or gas leaks. Small wires embedded in the stem in turn support the filament and its lead wires.

An electric current heats the filament to typically 2,000 to 3,300K (1,730 to 3,030°C; 3,140 to 5,480°F), well below tungsten's melting point of 3,695K (3,422°C; 6,191°F). Filament temperatures depend on the filament type, shape, size, and amount of current drawn. The heated filament emits light that approximates a [continuous spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_body "Black body"). The useful part of the emitted energy is [visible light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_light "Visible light"), but most energy is given off as heat in the near-[infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared "Infrared") wavelengths.

### Bulbs

Most light bulbs have either clear or coated glass. Coated glass bulbs have [kaolin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaolin "Kaolin") clay blown in and electrostatically deposited on the interior of the bulb. The powder layer diffuses the light from the filament. Pigments may be added to the clay to adjust the color of the light emitted. Kaolin diffused bulbs are used extensively in interior lighting because of their comparatively gentle light. Other kinds of colored bulbs are also made, including the various colors used for "party bulbs", [Christmas tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas_tree) lights and other decorative lighting. These are created by [coloring the glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass_coloring_and_color_marking "Glass coloring and color marking") with a [dopant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dopant "Dopant"); which is often a metal like [cobalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobalt "Cobalt") (blue) or [chromium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromium "Chromium") (green). Neodymium-containing glass is sometimes used to provide a more natural-appearing light.

The glass bulb of a general service lamp can reach temperatures between 200 and 260°C (392 and 500°F). Lamps intended for high power operation or used for heating purposes will have envelopes made of hard glass or [fused quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fused_quartz "Fused quartz").<sup>[77]</sup>

If a light bulb envelope leaks, the hot tungsten filament reacts with air, yielding an aerosol of brown [tungsten nitride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten_nitride "Tungsten nitride"), brown [tungsten dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten_dioxide "Tungsten dioxide"), violet-blue [tungsten pentoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten_pentoxide "Tungsten pentoxide"), and yellow [tungsten trioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten_trioxide "Tungsten trioxide") that then gets deposited on the nearby surfaces or the bulb interior.

### Gas fill

Most modern bulbs are filled with an [inert gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inert_gas "Inert gas") to reduce [evaporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporation "Evaporation") of the filament and prevent its [oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation "Oxidation"). The gas is at a pressure of about 70kPa (0.7atm).<sup>[105]</sup>

The gas reduces evaporation of the filament, but the fill must be chosen carefully to avoid introducing significant heat losses. For these properties, chemical inertness and high [atomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_atomic_mass "Relative atomic mass") or [molecular weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_mass "Molecular mass") is desirable. The presence of gas keeps the tungsten below its vapor pressure, allowing it to be operated at higher temperature without reducing its life (or, for operating at the same temperature, prolongs the filament life). On the other hand, the presence of the gas leads to heat loss from the filament—and therefore efficiency loss due to reduced incandescence—by [heat conduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_conduction "Heat conduction") and [heat convection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_convection "Heat convection").

Early lamps used only a vacuum to protect the filament from oxygen. The vacuum increases evaporation of the filament but eliminates two modes of heat loss. Some small modern lamps use vacuum as well.

The most commonly used fills are:<sup>[106]</sup>

- [Vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum), used in small lamps. Provides best [thermal insulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_insulation "Thermal insulation") of the filament but does not protect against its evaporation. Used also in larger lamps where the outer bulb surface temperature has to be limited.
- [Argon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon) (93%) and [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen "Nitrogen") (7%), where argon is used for its inertness, low [thermal conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conductivity "Thermal conductivity") and low cost, and the nitrogen is added to increase the breakdown voltage and prevent arcing between parts of the filament.<sup>[105]</sup>
- Nitrogen, used in some higher-power lamps, e.g. projection lamps, and where higher breakdown voltage is needed due to proximity of filament parts or lead-in wires.
- [Krypton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krypton), which is more advantageous than argon due to its higher atomic weight and lower thermal conductivity (which also allows use of smaller bulbs), but its use is hindered by much higher cost, confining it mostly to smaller-size bulbs.
- Krypton mixed with [xenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenon "Xenon"), where xenon improves the gas properties further due to its higher atomic weight. Its use is however limited by its very high cost. The improvements by using xenon are modest in comparison to its cost.
- [Hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen), in special flashing lamps where rapid filament cooling is required; its high thermal conductivity is exploited here.
- [Halogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogen), a small amount mixed with inert gas which is used in halogen lamps, a distinct type of incandescent lamp.

The gas fill must be free of traces of water, which greatly accelerates bulb blackening (see below).

The gas layer close to the filament (called the Langmuir layer) is stagnant, with heat transfer occurring only by conduction. Only at some distance does convection occur to carry heat to the bulb's envelope.

The orientation of the filament influences efficiency. Gas flow parallel to the filament, e.g., a vertically oriented bulb with vertical (or axial) filament, reduces convective losses.

The efficiency of the lamp increases with a larger filament diameter. Thin-filament, low-power bulbs benefit less from a fill gas, so are often only evacuated.

Early light bulbs with carbon filaments also used [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide "Carbon monoxide"), [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen "Nitrogen"), or [mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_(element) "Mercury (element)") vapor. However, carbon filaments operate at lower temperatures than tungsten ones, so the effect of the fill gas was not significant as the heat losses offset any benefits.

## Manufacturing

[![](http://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/00/Tantalum_light_bulb.png/500px-Tantalum_light_bulb.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tantalum_light_bulb.png)

*The 1902 [tantalum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantalum "Tantalum") filament light bulb was the first one to have a metal filament. This one is from 1908.*

Early bulbs were laboriously assembled by hand. After automatic machinery was developed, the cost of bulbs fell. Until 1910, when Libbey's Westlake machine went into production, bulbs were generally produced by a team of three workers (two gatherers and a master gaffer) blowing the bulbs into wooden or cast-iron molds, coated with a paste. Around 150 bulbs per hour were produced by the hand-blowing process in the 1880s at Corning Glass Works.

The Westlake machine, developed by [Libbey Glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libbey_Glass), was based on an adaptation of the Owens-Libbey bottle-blowing machine. [Corning Glass Works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corning_Glass_Works) soon began developing competing automated bulb-blowing machines, the first of which to be used in production was the E-Machine.

### Ribbon machine

Corning continued developing automated bulb-production machines, installing the Ribbon Machine in 1926 in its [Wellsboro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellsboro,_Pennsylvania "Wellsboro, Pennsylvania"), Pennsylvania, factory. The Ribbon Machine surpassed any previous attempts to automate bulb production and was used to produce incandescent bulbs into the 21st century. The inventor, William Woods, along with his colleague at Corning Glass Works, David E. Gray, had created a machine that by 1939 was producing 1,000 bulbs per minute.

The Ribbon Machine works by passing a continuous ribbon of glass along a [conveyor belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conveyor_belt "Conveyor belt"), heated in a furnace, and then blown by precisely aligned air nozzles through holes in the conveyor belt into molds. Thus the glass bulbs or envelopes are created. A typical machine can produce 50,000 to 120,000 bulbs per hour, depending on the size of the bulb. By the 1970s, 15 ribbon machines installed in factories around the world produced the entire supply of incandescent bulbs. The filament and its supports are assembled on a glass stem, which is then fused to the bulb. The air is pumped out of the bulb, and the evacuation tube in the stem press is sealed by a flame. The bulb is then inserted into the lamp base, and the whole assembly tested. The 2016 closing of [Osram-Sylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osram_Sylvania "Osram Sylvania")'s Wellsboro, Pennsylvania plant meant that one of the last remaining ribbon machines in the United States was shut down.

## Filament

*How a tungsten filament is made*

[Carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon) has the highest melting point of any element, and in [carbon arc lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_arc_lamp "Carbon arc lamp") it had been demonstrated to produce incandescence fairly close to that of sunlight. However, carbon has a tendency to [sublimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublimation_(phase_transition) "Sublimation (phase transition)") before reaching its melting point depending on pressure, which led to rapid blackening of vacuumed bulbs. The first commercially successful light bulb filaments were made from carbonized paper or [bamboo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bamboo "Bamboo"). Carbon filaments have a negative [temperature coefficient of resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature_coefficient_of_resistivity "Temperature coefficient of resistivity")—as they get hotter, their electrical resistance decreases. This made the lamp sensitive to fluctuations in the power supply, since a small increase of voltage would cause the filament to heat up, reducing its resistance and causing it to draw even more power and heat even further.

Carbon filaments were "flashed" by heating in a hydrocarbon vapor (usually gasoline), to improve their strength and uniformity. Metallized or "graphitized" filaments were first heated to high temperature to transform them into [graphite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite "Graphite"), which further strengthened and smoothed the filament. These filaments have a positive temperature coefficient, like a metallic [conductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conductor_(electrical) "Conductor (electrical)"), which stabilized the lamps operating properties against minor variations in supply voltage.

Metal filaments were tried in 1897 and started to displace carbon starting around 1904. Tungsten has the highest available melting point, but brittleness was an obstacle. By 1910, a process was developed by [William D. Coolidge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_D._Coolidge) at [General Electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric) for production of a ductile form of tungsten. The process required pressing tungsten powder into bars, then several steps of sintering, [swaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swaging "Swaging"), and then wire drawing. It was found that very pure tungsten formed filaments that sagged in use, and that a very small "doping" treatment with potassium, silicon, and aluminum oxides at the level of a few hundred parts per million (so-called AKS tungsten) greatly improved the life and durability of the tungsten filaments.

The predominant mechanism for failure in tungsten filaments even now is [grain boundary sliding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_boundary_sliding "Grain boundary sliding") accommodated by diffusional creep.<sup>[114]</sup> During operation, the tungsten wire is stressed under the load of its own weight and because of the diffusion that can occur at high temperatures, grains begin to rotate and slide. This stress, because of variations in the filament, causes the filament to sag nonuniformly, which ultimately introduces further torque on the filament.<sup>[114]</sup> It is this sagging that inevitably results in a rupture of the filament, rendering the incandescent lightbulb useless.<sup>[114]</sup>

### Coiled coil filament

To improve the efficiency of the lamp, the filament usually consists of multiple coils of coiled fine wire, also known as a ***coiled coil***. Light bulbs using coiled coil filaments are sometimes referred to as *double-coil bulbs*. For a 60-watt 120-volt lamp, the uncoiled length of the tungsten filament is usually 580 millimetres (22.8in), and the filament diameter is 0.046 millimetres (0.0018in). The advantage of the coiled coil is that there is less flow of gas in the bulb along the surface of the filament wire itself. Instead, gas only flows around the outside of whole coiled coil form, reducing both evaporation of the material and heat loss from the wire. The coiled-coil filament evaporates more slowly than a straight filament of the same surface area and light-emitting power, meaning the bulb lasts longer. The increased heat retained in the filament means that it runs hotter, which results in a more efficient light source.

### Reducing filament evaporation

During ordinary operation, the tungsten of the filament evaporates; hotter, more-efficient filaments evaporate faster. Because of this, the lifetime of a filament lamp is a trade-off between efficiency and longevity. The trade-off is typically set to provide a lifetime of 1,000 to 2,000 hours for lamps used for general illumination. Theatrical, photographic, and projection lamps may have a useful life of only a few hours, trading life expectancy for high output in a compact form. Long-life general service lamps have lower efficiency, but prior to the development of compact fluorescent and LED lamps they were useful in applications where the bulb was difficult to change.

[Irving Langmuir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_Langmuir) found that an inert gas, instead of vacuum, would retard evaporation. General service incandescent light bulbs over about 25 watts in rating are now filled with a mixture of mostly [argon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon "Argon") and some [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen "Nitrogen"), or sometimes [krypton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krypton "Krypton"). While inert gas reduces filament evaporation, it also conducts heat from the filament, thereby cooling the filament and reducing efficiency. At constant pressure and temperature, the thermal conductivity of a gas depends upon the molecular weight of the gas and the cross sectional area of the gas molecules. Higher molecular weight gases have lower thermal conductivity, because both the molecular weight and cross sectional area are higher. [Xenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenon) gas improves efficiency because of its high molecular weight, but is also more expensive, so its use is limited to smaller lamps.

*Filament notching* is due to uneven evaporation of the filament. Small variations in [resistivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistivity "Electrical resistivity") along the filament cause "hot spots" to form at points of higher resistivity; a variation of diameter of only 1% will cause a 25% reduction in service life. Since filament resistance is highly temperature-dependent, spots with higher temperature will have higher resistance, causing them to dissipate more energy, making them hotter – a [positive feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_feedback "Positive feedback") loop. These hot spots evaporate faster than the rest of the filament, permanently increasing the resistance at that point. The process ends in the familiar tiny gap in an otherwise healthy-looking filament.

Lamps operated on direct current develop random stairstep irregularities on the filament surface which may cut lifespan in half compared to AC operation; different alloys of tungsten and [rhenium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhenium "Rhenium") can be used to counteract the effect.<sup>[120]</sup><sup>[121]</sup>

Since a filament breaking in a gas-filled bulb can form an [electric arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_arc "Electric arc"), which may spread between the terminals and draw very heavy current, intentionally thin lead-in wires or more elaborate protection devices are therefore often used as [fuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuse_(electrical) "Fuse (electrical)") built into the light bulb.<sup>[122]</sup> More nitrogen is used in higher-voltage lamps to reduce the possibility of arcing.<sup>[117]</sup>

### Bulb blackening

In a conventional lamp, the evaporated tungsten condenses on the inner surface of the glass envelope, darkening it. For bulbs that contain a vacuum, the darkening is uniform across the entire surface of the envelope. When a filling of inert gas is used, the evaporated tungsten is carried in the thermal convection currents of the gas, and is deposited preferentially on the uppermost part of the envelope, blackening just that portion of the envelope. Study of the problem of bulb blackening led to the discovery of [thermionic emission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermionic_emission "Thermionic emission"), the invention of the [vacuum tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_tube "Vacuum tube"), and [evaporation deposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporation_(deposition) "Evaporation (deposition)") used to make [mirrors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror "Mirror") and other [optical coatings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin-film_optics "Thin-film optics").

A very small amount of water vapor inside a light bulb can significantly increase lamp darkening. Water vapor [dissociates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermolysis "Thermolysis") into hydrogen and oxygen at the hot filament. The oxygen attacks the tungsten metal, and the resulting tungsten oxide particles travel to cooler parts of the lamp. Hydrogen from water vapor reduces the oxide, reforming water vapor and continuing this *water cycle*. The equivalent of a drop of water distributed over 500,000 lamps will significantly increase darkening. Small amounts of substances such as [zirconium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium "Zirconium") are placed within the lamp as a [getter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getter "Getter") to react with any oxygen that may bake out of the lamp components during operation.

Some old, high-powered lamps used in theater, projection, searchlight, and lighthouse service with heavy, sturdy filaments contained loose tungsten powder within the envelope. From time to time, the operator would remove the bulb and shake it, allowing the tungsten powder to scrub off most of the tungsten that had condensed on the interior of the envelope, removing the blackening and brightening the lamp again.<sup>[127]</sup>

### Halogen lamps

Main article: [Halogen lamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogen_lamp)

[![](http://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Tungsten_filament_in_halogen_lamp.JPG/500px-Tungsten_filament_in_halogen_lamp.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tungsten_filament_in_halogen_lamp.JPG)

*Close-up of a tungsten filament inside a [halogen lamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogen_lamp "Halogen lamp"). The two ring-shaped structures left and right are filament supports.*

The [halogen lamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogen_lamp "Halogen lamp") reduces uneven evaporation of the filament and eliminates darkening of the envelope by filling the lamp with a [halogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogen "Halogen") gas at low pressure, along with an inert gas. The [halogen cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogen_cycle "Halogen cycle") increases the lifetime of the bulb and prevents its darkening by redepositing tungsten from the inside of the bulb back onto the filament. The halogen lamp can operate its filament at a higher temperature than a standard gas filled lamp of similar power without loss of operating life. Such bulbs are much smaller than normal incandescent bulbs, and are widely used where intense illumination is needed in a limited space. [Fiber-optic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_fiber "Optical fiber") lamps for [optical microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_microscopy "Optical microscopy") is one typical application.

### Incandescent arc lamps

A variation of the incandescent lamp did not use a hot wire filament, but instead used an arc struck on a spherical bead electrode to produce heat. The electrode then became incandescent, with the arc contributing little to the light produced. Such lamps were used for projection or illumination for scientific instruments such as [microscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscope "Microscope"). These arc lamps ran on relatively low voltages and incorporated tungsten filaments to start [ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization "Ionization") within the envelope. They provided the intense concentrated light of an [arc lamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_lamp "Arc lamp") but were easier to operate. Developed around 1915, these lamps were displaced by mercury and [xenon arc lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenon_arc_lamp "Xenon arc lamp").

### Power

Incandescent lamps are nearly pure resistive loads with a [power factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_factor "Power factor") of 1. Unlike discharge lamps or LED lamps, the power consumed is equal to the [apparent power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_power "Apparent power") in the circuit. Incandescent light bulbs are usually [marketed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing "Marketing") according to the [electrical power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power "Electric power") consumed. This depends mainly on the operating [resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistance "Electrical resistance") of the filament. For two bulbs of the same voltage, and type, the higher-powered bulb gives more light.

The table shows the approximate typical output, in [lumens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumen_(unit) "Lumen (unit)"), of standard 120 volt incandescent light bulbs at various powers. Light output of similar 230V bulbs is usually slightly less, even though in this table the opposite holds true at 25W. The lower current (higher voltage) filament is thinner and has to be operated at a slightly lower temperature for the same life expectancy, which reduces [energy efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efficient_energy_use "Efficient energy use").<sup>[133]</sup>

The lumen values for "soft white" bulbs will generally be slightly lower than for clear bulbs at the same power.

### Current and resistance

The resistance of the filament is temperature dependent. The cold resistance of tungsten-filament lamps is about  the resistance when operating. For example, a 100-watt, 120-volt lamp has a resistance of 144[ohms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohm "Ohm") when lit, but the cold resistance is much lower (about 9.5ohms). Since incandescent lamps are resistive loads, simple phase-control [TRIAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRIAC) [dimmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimmer "Dimmer") can be used to control brightness. Electrical contacts may carry a "T" rating symbol indicating that they are designed to control circuits with the high inrush current characteristic of tungsten lamps. For a 100-watt, 120-volt general-service lamp, the current stabilizes in about 0.10 seconds, and the lamp reaches 90% of its full brightness after about 0.13 seconds.

## Physical characteristics

### Safety

The filament in a tungsten light bulb is not easy to break when the bulb is cold, but filaments are more vulnerable when they are hot because the incandescent metal is less rigid. An impact on the outside of the bulb may cause the filament to break or experience a surge in [electric current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current "Electric current") that causes part of it to melt or vaporize. In most modern incandescent bulbs, part of the wire inside the bulb acts like a [fuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuse_(electrical) "Fuse (electrical)"): if a broken filament produces an [electrical short](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_short "Electrical short") inside the bulb, the fusible section of wire will melt and cut the current off to prevent damage to the supply lines.

A hot glass bulb may fracture on contact with cold objects. When the glass envelope breaks, the bulb [implodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implosion_(mechanical_process) "Implosion (mechanical process)"), exposing the filament to ambient air. The air then usually destroys the hot filament through [oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation "Oxidation").

## Light output and lifetime

See also: [Lamp rerating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamp_rerating)

Incandescent lamps are very sensitive to changes in the supply voltage. These characteristics are of great practical and economic importance.

For a supply voltage *V* near the rated voltage of the lamp:

- *Light* output is approximately proportional to *V*<sup>3.4</sup>
- *Power* consumption is approximately proportional to *V*<sup>1.6</sup>
- *Lifetime* is approximately proportional to *V*<sup>−16</sup>
- *Color temperature* is approximately proportional to *V*<sup>0.42</sup><sup>[116]</sup>

A 5% reduction in voltage will double the life of the bulb, but reduce its light output by about 16%. Long-life bulbs take advantage of this trade-off in applications such as traffic signal lamps. Since electric energy they use costs more than the cost of the bulb, general service lamps emphasize efficiency over long operating life. The objective is to minimize the cost of light, not the cost of lamps. Early bulbs had a life of up to 2500 hours, but in 1924 the [Phoebus cartel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoebus_cartel) agreed to limit life to 1000 hours. When this was exposed in 1953, General Electric and other leading American manufacturers were banned from limiting the life.

The relationships above are valid for only a few percent change of voltage around standard rated conditions, but they indicate that a lamp operated at low voltage could last much longer than at rated voltage, albeit with greatly reduced light output. The "[Centennial Light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centennial_Light)" is a light bulb that is accepted by the  as having been burning almost continuously at a [fire station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_station "Fire station") in [Livermore, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livermore,_California), since 1901. However, the bulb emits the equivalent light of a four watt bulb. A similar story can be told of a 40-watt bulb in Texas that has been illuminated since 21 September 1908. It once resided in an [opera house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera_house "Opera house") where notable celebrities stopped to take in its glow, and was moved to an area museum in 1977.

[Photoflood lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoflood "Photoflood") used for photographic lighting favor light output over life, with some lasting only two hours. The upper temperature limit for the filament is the melting point of the metal. Tungsten is the metal with the highest melting point, 3,695K (3,422°C; 6,191°F). A 50-hour-life projection bulb, for instance, is designed to operate only 50°C (122°F) below that melting point. Such a lamp may achieve up to 22 lumens per watt, compared with 17.5 for a 750-hour general service lamp.<sup>[77]</sup>

Lamps of the same power rating but designed for different voltages have different luminous efficacy. For example, a 100-watt, 1000-hour, 120-volt lamp will produce about 17.1 lumens per watt. A similar lamp designed for 230V would produce only around 12.8 lumens per watt, and one designed for 30 volts (train lighting) would produce as much as 19.8 lumens per watt.<sup>[77]</sup> Lower voltage lamps have a thicker filament, for the same power rating. They can run hotter for the same lifetime before the filament evaporates.

The wires used to support the filament make it mechanically stronger, but remove heat, creating another tradeoff between efficiency and long life. Many general-service 120-volt lamps use no additional support wires, but lamps designed for "[rough service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rough_service "Rough service")" or "vibration service" may have as many as five. Low-voltage lamps have filaments made of heavier wire and do not require additional support wires.

Very low voltages are inefficient since the lead wires would conduct too much heat away from the filament, so the practical lower limit for incandescent lamps is 1.5 volts. Very long filaments for high voltages are fragile, and lamp bases become more difficult to insulate, so lamps for illumination are not made with rated voltages over 300 volts.<sup>[77]</sup> Some infrared heating elements are made for higher voltages, but these use tubular bulbs with widely separated terminals.
